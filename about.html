Summary

I am a Professor in the Department of Electronics & Communication Engineering at Sri Ramanujar Engineering College, Chennai, and I have spent my career in two tracks that turned out to sharpen each other.

My field is VLSI Design. It runs continuously from the B.E. through the M.Tech and Ph.D. in VLSI Design, through seventeen published papers on CNTFET-based memory cells and low-power digital design, into the classroom — where I teach ASIC design, low-power design, solid state devices, VLSI design techniques, testing of VLSI circuits and CAD for VLSI at postgraduate level, and coordinate the ME VLSI programme and its laboratories.

In academia, I have moved through the full departmental hierarchy — Lecturer, Assistant Professor, Associate Professor, Head of Department, and now Professor & Head of Department — across four institutions since 2005. Alongside teaching I have carried the department: three years as Head of Department, two as Assistant Head, Chief Superintendent for Anna University examinations, NBA accreditation coordinator, project coordinator for UG and PG work, PG coordinator for the ME VLSI Design programme, and examination cell in-charge.

In industry I have spent seven years as a hands-on engineer and a delivery leader: a design engineer, then an iOS and Android developer, and finally Product Manager. That meant owning real code and real releases — designing architectures, meeting store requirements, planning sprints, and being accountable when a version actually shipped.

The combination is unusual, and it is the point. I can design a memory cell and simulate it in H-SPICE, and I can design a service architecture. I can mentor a student through their first VLSI design, and I can run a sprint that a paying customer depends on.

Alongside both tracks I work as an AI trainer and developer, using Claude, Cursor, Gemini and ChatGPT in practice rather than in theory. I run workshops for students and faculty, and bring AI-assisted development into real engineering work. What I emphasise is verification — these tools produce confident, plausible and occasionally wrong output, and a student who cannot check a generated Verilog module has not learned anything.
